## Part G — Unit economics and the LLM cost floor

### 21. When does a heavy customer become unprofitable, and what pricing survives it?

**Answer.** The COGS driver is tokens per review times reviews per month, plus a fixed hosting floor (Container Apps + SQL + monitoring). The dangerous shape is the flat-tier heavy user: a Professional-tier tenant running many multi-agent Real-mode reviews per day consumes token spend that scales linearly while revenue stays flat — the classic AI-SaaS margin inversion. The structures that survive: (a) **metered packages** — the billing noun is already "packages/month" (Done TB-743), which is the right unit because it maps one-to-one to token cost; (b) **fair-use ceilings on flat tiers** with metered overage, disclosed honestly rather than hidden in terms; (c) **mode-differentiated pricing** — Simulator/draft iterations cheap or free, Real-mode finalizing runs metered, which also nudges the workflow toward the committed-package behavior the product wants. What makes the answer non-hypothetical: the non-bypassable LLM accounting chokepoint (**M-225/TB-1287**) and the `(RunId, TaskId)` idempotent billing work (**M-170/M-171**) mean per-review COGS is (or will be) *measurable per tenant* — but no owner-facing unit-economics model currently turns that telemetry into a pricing floor and a heavy-user policy. That's the gap: the plumbing exists, the P&L view doesn't.

**Backlog disposition:** Plumbing covered — **M-225/M-226 + TB-1287/TB-1288**, **M-170/M-171 + TB-1020/TB-1021**, **M-200 + TB-1166–TB-1170** (tier repricing). New: **GQ-09** — per-review unit-economics model + heavy-user fair-use policy (§21).

### 22. If a competitor sells "good enough" single-pass reviews at a third of the cost, how does the premium defend itself commercially?

**Answer.** Not on analysis quality — that race goes to the cheapest acceptable output. The commercial defense has three legs. **First, price the artifact, not the inference:** a third-cheaper single-pass review produces a cheaper *answer*; it does not produce a signed, comparable, auditable *package*. As long as the meter says "finalized architecture package" and the buyer's forcing function (audit, ARB, sponsor) demands the package properties, the token-cost differential is invisible in the price the buyer compares. **Second, keep COGS honest inside the pipeline:** the multi-agent premium must be spent only where it buys provenance or measured lift (first batch, Q12/GQ-01) — stages that add tokens without adding evidence should collapse, which directly narrows the competitor's cost advantage. **Third, segment retreat:** concede the ungoverned segment explicitly. Buyers who want cheap advisory output are the "raw AI is sufficient" segment from Q1 — the honest qualification already says don't fight there. The failure mode to avoid: matching the competitor's price while keeping the pipeline's cost structure, which converts a positioning problem into a margin problem.

**Backlog disposition:** Covered by **GQ-01** (ablation → collapse unearned stages), **GQ-09** (COGS visibility), evidence-package-first vocabulary (Done TB-738–TB-747). No additional items.

### 23. Inference prices drop 10× — or rise 3×. Which is more dangerous?

**Answer.** **The 10× drop is more dangerous, and it isn't close.** A 3× rise hurts margin arithmetic but is survivable and even mildly favorable competitively: ArchLucid's costs rise, but so do every competitor's, and expensive inference *raises* the relative value of doing fewer, better-governed runs — a governance product tolerates dear tokens better than a volume chat product does. Mitigations are ordinary: metering (GQ-09), throttle policy (**M-229**), budget gates. The 10× drop is dangerous because it collapses the *labor-substitution anchor* from Q11: when everyone's AI analysis is nearly free, "AI findings" become ambient — every wiki, IDE, and ticket system will embed them — and the only defensible price is for what stays scarce: the governed record, the gates, the audit trail. That accelerates the commoditization timeline from the first batch's Q3/Q19 and compresses the window in which "our pipeline produces better findings" carries any price at all. Practical consequence: pricing should already be migrating its justification from analysis cost toward governance value, so a price collapse in the former doesn't strand the rate card. The sensitivity belongs in the unit-economics model, and the strategic watch belongs in the commoditization ledger.

**Backlog disposition:** Fold into **GQ-09** (price-shock sensitivity rows) and **GQ-03** (commoditization watch, first batch). No standalone item.

## Part H — Platform dependency and concentration risk

### 24. "You've concentrated vendor risk in one hyperscaler" — steelman, and is multi-cloud analysis without multi-cloud hosting coherent?

**Answer.** **Steelman:** hosting, identity, models, and data all sit in one vendor's cloud under one commercial relationship (ADR 0020, Azure-primary, permanent). An Azure regional failure, a Microsoft commercial-terms change, or an AOAI policy shift each hit ArchLucid with no diversification; a buyer standardized on AWS also inherits a cloud relationship they didn't choose. **Assessment:** the position is coherent — and stronger than it sounds — *if argued precisely*. The subject of analysis and the substrate of hosting are independent axes: an accounting firm audits companies it doesn't run its ledger on. ArchLucid **analyzes** Azure, AWS, and GCP evidence (cloud connectors are optional; evidence-only reviews work with zero cloud access, per `CORE_PILOT.md`); it **runs** on Azure. The buyer-relevant questions are data protection, exit rights, and availability — answered by the extractor's no-tenant-access posture, offline-verifiable exports, and the DR posture in `SLA_SUMMARY.md` — not by hosting plurality. Where it's genuinely *incoherent* is when a buyer surface muddles the two axes ("multi-cloud" ambiguity), and the confusion risk is real: the AWS/GCP analysis-parity work (**TB-874–TB-876**) must never be readable as multi-cloud *hosting*. Buyers for whom Azure-anything is a hard blocker are already an explicit not-a-fit (`BUYER_PERSONAS.md`).

**Backlog disposition:** Mostly covered — ADR 0020, not-a-fit anchors, **TB-874–TB-876**. New: **GQ-10** — hyperscaler-concentration objection talk track that separates the two axes explicitly (§21).

### 25. What breaks — technically and contractually — when the model that produced a finding no longer exists?

**Answer.** This has a precise, already-mapped answer (`docs/library/AOAI_MODEL_RETIREMENT_REPRO_CLAIM_MAP.md`, **M-273/TB-1499**). **Survives retirement:** committed golden manifests and `ManifestHash` (hash over sealed content, no LLM call); export lineage and `/export/verify`; comparison replay from stored sources (`ComparisonRecords`, persisted `AgentResults` — no AOAI re-call); Simulator/offline cohort baselines. **Breaks:** any claim implying bit-identical *re-execution* of Real agents on the historical pin — new completions on a new model version can't reproduce old outputs, and Microsoft's auto-upgrade default makes this **quietly** false rather than loudly false (no-auto-upgrade deployments instead fail hard with 410); live Real-cohort gates on the retired pin; fine-tuned deployments on retired bases (own retirement schedule); embedding re-index retrieval stability. **Contractually:** nothing in the buyer promise should ever have depended on re-execution — the safe pin is "packages remain hash-verifiable forever relative to stored bytes; re-running the old model is not promised." The residual operational risk is the silent auto-upgrade path: model change without the deliberate re-baseline ritual, so eval baselines drift without anyone naming the cause — exactly the rubber-stamp re-lock that **TB-1172/TB-1173** forbids.

**Backlog disposition:** Fully covered — **M-273/M-274 + TB-1499/TB-1500**, **M-201/M-202 + TB-1172/TB-1173**, claim map doc. No new items.

### 26. Microsoft ships native architecture review (Advisor, Copilot for Azure, WAF tooling) — distribution advantage or kill zone?

**Answer.** Both, split by layer. **Where Microsoft crushes:** Azure-resource-level recommendations — Advisor, WAF assessments, Copilot-surfaced best practices. They own the telemetry, the console, and the bundle price of "free with your EA." Any ArchLucid finding of the form "this Azure resource violates this Azure best practice" is in the kill zone today, regardless of quality. **Where Microsoft structurally won't go:** cross-cloud and evidence-only review (Microsoft tooling reviews *Azure*, not your AWS estate or your architecture brief); vendor-neutral governed decision records (an auditor may reasonably discount the fox's report on the henhouse — Microsoft grading Microsoft workloads has an inherent conflict ArchLucid doesn't); SoD approval workflow and append-only decision ledgers, which are workflow products Microsoft historically leaves to partners; and packaging for *non-Azure* audiences (procurement, exec sponsors). **Net:** Azure-native is a distribution advantage (Marketplace, co-sell motion, buyer comfort) precisely *because* the product's differentiated layer sits above the resource-recommendation layer Microsoft owns. The discipline required: findings must keep citing *the customer's evidence and policy packs*, not re-derivable Azure best practices — every finding reproducible by free Advisor is a finding that shouldn't headline a demo. This belongs as a standing entry in the commoditization watch.

**Backlog disposition:** Add Microsoft-native tooling to **GQ-03** (commoditization watch) scope; talk-track leg lands in **GQ-10**. No standalone engineering item.

## Part I — Liability, IP, and professional responsibility

### 27. A wrong compliance finding leads to a regulatory penalty — where does liability land, and what contract language is realistic?

**Answer.** **Where it lands:** overwhelmingly on the enterprise, then on the accountable human approver inside it — regulators penalize the regulated party, not its software vendors. The vendor's exposure is derivative: a contract claim (breach of warranty, misrepresentation) if the product promised more than "decision support," or a negligence theory if marketing implied certification. This is why the product's existing posture — human accountability preserved, no automated compliance sign-off (`COMPETITIVE_POSITIONING.md`), no pack-as-certification (**M-235**) — is not just honesty but the *primary liability shield*: every claim-honesty row doubles as legal-risk reduction. **What a vendor this size can offer:** liability capped at fees paid (12 months is the standard ask), express disclaimer that findings are advisory inputs to customer judgment, no indemnity for customer's regulatory outcomes, warranty limited to material conformance with documentation. **What buyers will demand:** uncapped or super-capped liability for data breach and IP infringement (market-standard and probably conceded), and occasionally indemnity for "errors in output" (must be refused — no AI vendor of any size grants it; pointing at OpenAI/Microsoft's own terms is the effective rebuttal). The gap: no order-form liability schedule or E&O insurance posture currently exists in the repo's commercial docs.

**Backlog disposition:** Honesty half covered — **M-235/M-236**, **M-239/M-240**, `WHAT_NOT_TO_PROMISE`. New: **GQ-11** — liability schedule + E&O posture for the order form (§21).

### 28. Does productizing professional judgment create E&O exposure a license disclaimer can't contain?

**Answer.** The exposure is real but containable — *if* the product never crosses the line from "tool the professional uses" to "professional the buyer relies on." Precedents run both directions: CAD software, static analyzers, and tax-prep software all productized judgment-adjacent work behind enforceable disclaimers; the disclaimers failed mainly where marketing promised outcomes ("guaranteed compliant") or where the product presented conclusions without visible reasoning for the professional to check. ArchLucid sits on the defensible side of both tests *by architecture*: findings carry evidence anchors and confidence limits (the professional can inspect the basis), a human finalizes every package, and SoD gates make the human accountability structural rather than performative. The two things a disclaimer genuinely can't contain: (a) **marketing drift** — one "ensures compliance" in a datasheet undermines every carefully-worded term; the over-promise matrix (**M-239**) is the standing defense; (b) **the reliance pattern of small customers** — a sophisticated enterprise architect is a learned intermediary; a two-person startup treating the output as their compliance department is not, which argues for keeping the ICP enterprise-shaped. Practical residual: carry E&O/professional-liability insurance anyway — it's cheap relative to the tail risk, and its absence is itself a procurement question (folds into GQ-11).

**Backlog disposition:** Structural defenses shipped or in flight (**M-207**, SoD, confidence limits). Contract/insurance leg: **GQ-11**. No engineering items.

### 29. Who owns the IP in a package generated from customer evidence by a vendor pipeline on Microsoft's models?

**Answer.** Four layers, three of them clean: (1) **customer evidence in** — customer's, always; the DPA already governs processing. (2) **The generated package** — should be assigned to or licensed perpetually to the customer; any other answer kills deals, since the package's entire purpose is to be *their* defensible record. (3) **The pipeline, agents, prompts, templates, policy-pack structure** — vendor's, standard. (4) **Model-output copyrightability and provider terms** — the genuinely murky layer: Azure OpenAI terms assign output rights to the *service customer*, which is ArchLucid, so a pass-through grant to the end customer must be explicit in ArchLucid's own terms; and pure AI-generated content has contested copyright status in several jurisdictions (mitigated by the fact that a *finalized* package includes human-approved selection and arrangement). **What a sharp procurement lawyer attacks:** silence — and the repo's commercial docs are currently silent; the `DPA_TEMPLATE.md` contains no ownership, escrow, or output-rights language. Second attack: whether ArchLucid uses customer evidence or outputs to improve models cross-tenant (the answer must be an explicit no, stated in terms, not just implied by trust-center posture). Third: aggregated/derived data rights (benchmarks, calibration stats — GQ-02 makes this live), which need a carve-out defined *before* a lawyer finds it undefined.

**Backlog disposition:** New — **GQ-12**: output-IP ownership + no-cross-tenant-training + derived-data clauses for order form/DPA + trust-center FAQ (§21). Owner/legal work; no engineering items.

## Part J — Channel, ecosystem, and the consulting question

### 30. Consultancies: natural channel or motivated saboteurs?

**Answer.** Both, segmented by what the firm actually sells. **Saboteurs:** firms whose margin *is* the review hours — the boutique whose deliverable is a 60-page architecture assessment sees ArchLucid as deflationary and will quietly poison evaluations they're asked to advise on ("we can do this more thoroughly by hand"). No partnership structure flips them; their P&L is the objection. **Natural channel:** firms that sell *transformation outcomes* and treat review as an unbillable-ish gate on the way — for them a tool that compresses review from days to hours raises project velocity and margin. The structure that flips a fence-sitter: position ArchLucid as the *consultant's leverage*, not their replacement — the firm runs reviews under its own brand, the evidence-linked package raises their deliverable quality above hand-built decks, per-package economics let them price the same deliverable with better margin. Concretely: a practitioner license or white-label-lite tier, co-branded exports, and the firm keeps the client relationship. The repo's `SERVICE_LED_OFFERS.md` already gestures at service-led motion but from the vendor's side; the partner-side thesis (their margin math, their objection map) is unwritten. One caution: channel conflict cuts both ways — direct pilots in accounts a partner considers theirs will end the partnership faster than any competitor.

**Backlog disposition:** New — **GQ-13**: consultancy/SI channel thesis memo (partner margin math, practitioner-tier sketch, conflict rules) (§21).

### 31. Sell to consultancies as internal tooling vs sell to enterprises directly — which compounds?

**Answer.** **Direct compounds; consultancy-first caps out — but consultancy revenue arrives sooner.** The consultancy motion's ceiling: the buyer pool is small (thousands of firms, few with tooling budgets), the buyer is sophisticated and margin-defensive (Q30), and the vendor is structurally hostage — the firm owns the end-customer relationship, aggregates demand into one negotiable contract, and can commission a lookalike once volume justifies it. Worse for this product specifically: the *evidence ledger's* compounding value (cross-review compare, portfolio history) accrues per *end customer*; a consultancy hopping between clients resets it every engagement. The direct motion's compounding: each enterprise tenant accumulates committed reviews, making the compare/audit moat deeper and churn costlier — the product's differentiation literally grows with tenure, which is the definition of a compounding motion. The honest synthesis for a solo vendor with runway pressure: direct-led with consultancies as an *opportunistic accelerant* — take practitioner-license revenue where it walks in (it also produces reference-quality usage fast), but never let the roadmap or pricing be captured by a channel whose interests diverge at scale. This is a strategy input to GQ-13, not a separate work item.

**Backlog disposition:** Folded into **GQ-13** (the memo must take a position on primary vs accelerant). No new items.

### 32. Which integrations are load-bearing, and which are checkbox theater a small vendor shouldn't have built?

**Answer.** **Load-bearing, in order:** (1) **SSO/Entra + SCIM-ish user lifecycle** — not stickiness but *admission*: without it the security review ends before value is discussed. (2) **Export formats** (DOCX/exec packet, offline-verifiable export) — the product's value *is* an artifact that travels; exports are the product, not an integration. (3) **ITSM outbound (Jira/ServiceNow create + status sync)** — genuinely load-bearing because it puts findings into the system where work is dispatched, which is what makes packages *cited in decisions* (the Q6/Q10 survival signal); the outbox/DLQ delivery-guarantee work (**M-280/TB-1530**) is what makes it trustworthy rather than decorative. **Defensible but watch the spend:** cloud connectors — optional by design (evidence-only first, per `CORE_PILOT.md`), valuable for configuration-backed findings, but three-cloud *parity* (TB-874–876) should track buyer pull, not symmetry aesthetics. **Closest to checkbox theater:** chat notifications (Teams/Slack) — nice demo chrome, weak retention causality, and they've already consumed a large P0 UX-hygiene tax (the not-configured empty-state clusters); webhooks partially redeem the category by being the generic escape hatch that lets customers build what the vendor didn't. The portfolio conclusion: nothing needs ripping out, but future integration effort should require a "which pilot metric does this move?" answer before it's built.

**Backlog disposition:** Covered — `INTEGRATION_CATALOG.md`, **M-280/M-281**, **M-257/M-258** (canonical not-configured empty state), existing UX clusters. No new items.

## Part K — The agentic future

### 33. Does request → review → finalize survive when agents make architecture decisions continuously?

**Answer.** The *cycle cadence* doesn't survive; the *commit semantics* do — and the distinction is the product's escape route. What breaks: the assumption that architecture changes arrive in human-sized, meeting-shaped batches worth a discrete review session. When coding and infra agents modify architecture continuously, a quarterly (or even weekly) review is auditing a river by photographing it. What survives — and appreciates: the notion of a **governed checkpoint**. Continuous change *increases* the need for moments where a state is sealed, evidenced, gated, and signed, because no human is watching the stream in real time; the committed golden manifest is exactly that checkpoint primitive. The evolution path is cadence, not architecture: review triggered by *change significance* (delta since last committed manifest crosses a threshold) rather than by calendar; gates invoked at agent-initiated checkpoints; humans reviewing exceptions rather than everything. Crucially, the expensive invariants already built — append-only audit, SoD, manifest identity, compare — are cadence-agnostic; what would need building is the trigger plane and API ergonomics for machine-initiated reviews. The strategic error to avoid is panic-pivoting to "agent governance platform" before any customer runs agents that way; the correct posture in 2026 is a positioning spike plus API-shape awareness, not a rebuild.

**Backlog disposition:** New — **GQ-14** (agent-era governance spike, explicitly V2-window; §21). Async-orchestration groundwork already tracked (**M-231/M-232 + TB-1311/TB-1312**).

### 34. Could the strongest future customer be an AI agent needing its decisions governed?

**Answer.** Plausibly yes — and it's the most favorable version of the agentic future for this product, because it converts the commoditization threat (Q4) into demand: the more decisions agents make, the more some enterprise function must answer "who approved what this agent did, on what evidence?" That is *literally* the existing product promise with a non-human requester. What "governance API for autonomous agents" would require beyond current V1: (1) **machine-grade identity and authority** — agent service principals with scoped, revocable permissions (the mid-run authority-revocation claim map, **M-282/TB-1537**, is accidentally foundational here); (2) **synchronous-ish gate verdicts** — an agent mid-plan needs allow/block/needs-human in seconds, a different latency contract than a human review session (Tier-3 latency posture would need a new tier); (3) **programmatic evidence submission** — agents attach their plan, diffs, and context as evidence without a wizard; (4) **escalation semantics** — "needs-human" routed with enough context for a fast human verdict; (5) **volume economics** — thousands of micro-checkpoints, which collides directly with the token-cost question (Q21) and demands a cheap policy-evaluation fast path with LLM analysis reserved for significant deltas. None of this is V1 work; all of it should constrain API design choices made now (versioned API, typed events, and authority model are the parts that would be expensive to retrofit).

**Backlog disposition:** Folded into **GQ-14** — the spike must include the "agent as requester" requirements list as API-design guardrails. No V1 items.

### 35. Is the pipeline more valuable exposed as a callable service (MCP), or does that unbundle the workflow?

**Answer.** Split the product at the commit boundary and the answer resolves cleanly. **Exposing analysis as a callable tool** ("review this, return findings") is self-unbundling: the caller keeps the workflow, ArchLucid becomes a swappable findings vendor competing on per-call price against frontier models — the exact commodity position the whole strategy avoids. **Exposing the governance plane** as the callable surface is the opposite: "submit evidence, request checkpoint, receive gate verdict, retrieve signed package" makes external agents *producers feeding the ledger* rather than consumers strip-mining the analysis. The ledger, identity, and audit trail stay inside; every call *deepens* the committed-history moat instead of bypassing it. Rule of thumb for any future MCP/tool surface: **never expose findings-without-commitment as a primitive.** If a tool call can extract the analytical value while skipping finalize, gates, and audit, it unbundles; if the valuable output only exists *as* a committed, verifiable artifact, exposure is distribution. There's also a defensive timing argument: agent frameworks will want an architecture-governance tool in their catalogs within the window; being the one that exists beats being the one that's pure. But the sequencing discipline from Q33 holds — API-shape awareness now, build on demand signal.

**Backlog disposition:** Folded into **GQ-14** (the spike's third deliverable: the expose/never-expose boundary, written down before any MCP surface is built). No new items.

## Part L — Operations, trust, and the solo-vendor reality

### 36. What does a credible security-operations story look like for a company of one — and where does it fall short of buyer assumptions?

**Answer.** Credible means **automation-heavy, evidence-backed, and honest about the human layer.** The strong story available today: managed-PaaS posture (Container Apps, Azure SQL — Microsoft patches the substrate), Prometheus/App Insights alerting with burn-rate rules, append-only audit for forensics, documented runbooks, ZAP scanning in the pipeline, and a trust center that shows rather than asserts. A solo vendor can honestly claim faster *decision* loops than an enterprise SOC (no ticket queue between detection and the person who can fix it) and should. **Where it falls short of what buyers assume:** (a) **24/7 human response** — buyers picture an on-call rotation; the truth is one person who sleeps, travels, and gets sick; alert-to-human latency has a single point of failure. (b) **Incident concurrency** — one incident during one sales trip is the whole capacity. (c) **Separation of duties in ops** — the developer, the deployer, and the incident responder are the same identity, which contradicts the SoD story the *product* sells; compensating controls (immutable audit of admin actions, break-glass logging) must carry that weight and be documented. (d) **Breach-notification wall-clock commitments** — anything contractual (72h GDPR-style) must be achievable by one human plus automation, and stated with that caveat. The mitigation isn't pretending otherwise — it's a written, buyer-visible honest-ops one-pager plus an incident-retainer arrangement (a contracted second responder) before the first Enterprise-tier signature, not after the first incident.

**Backlog disposition:** Partially covered — `SLA_SUMMARY.md`, `SUPPORT_POLICY.md`, trust-center, runbooks. New: **GQ-15** — solo-vendor honest security-operations one-pager + second-responder retainer decision (§21).

### 37. What SLAs can a solo vendor honestly sign, and when does honesty start losing deals?

**Answer.** The current structure is already close to the honest optimum: **99.9% availability as an engineering SLO target for Team/Professional (no credits), contractual credits Enterprise-only when negotiated** (`SLA_SUMMARY.md`) — that's the right shape because 99.9% *availability* is an automation property (managed PaaS, rolling deploys, health probes) a solo vendor can genuinely deliver, while aggressive *human-response* commitments are not. Honestly signable: 99.9% monthly availability with the LLM-provider carve-out; 72h maintenance notice; business-hours support response with a defined severity ladder; RPO/RTO per the documented backup/DR posture. Not honestly signable: 24/7 sev-1 human response in minutes, guaranteed named-engineer escalation, or multi-hour breach-notification clocks without the Q36 retainer. **When honesty starts losing:** rarely at pilot (pilot buyers trade SLA depth for speed and price), materially at Enterprise procurement — typically the second or third Enterprise-tier negotiation, when a security team runs a standard vendor-SLA checklist built for hundred-person vendors. The countermove is reframing, not matching: publish the *measured* availability history (let the SLI speak), offer credits as skin-in-the-game, and be plainly honest that support depth is the trade for the price and the founder-direct access. Deals lost purely on unmatchable support-org checkboxes were mostly unwinnable at this company size anyway — the tracked question is *which* checkbox actually killed each one, which belongs in the kill-risk register's procurement-stall indicator (first batch, GQ-06).

**Backlog disposition:** Covered — `SLA_SUMMARY.md`, `SUPPORT_POLICY.md`, **GQ-06** (procurement-stall tracking). Human-response honesty folds into **GQ-15**. No new items.

### 38. Minimum credible data-residency story for a single-region vendor — and what forces multi-region?

**Answer.** Minimum credible story has four planks, none requiring a second region: (1) **name the region plainly** — "customer data is stored and processed in [region]; model calls go to Azure OpenAI in [region(s)]" on the trust center and in the DPA, with the AOAI processing geography stated separately because sharp reviewers ask about it separately; (2) **no silent geography changes** — contractual commitment that processing regions don't move without notice; (3) **exit with data** — offline-verifiable export means residency anxiety is bounded by an offboarding path the buyer can execute unilaterally (the **M-267** primitive again earning its keep); (4) **honest roadmap language** — "additional regions when demand warrants," never implying a capability that doesn't exist (the `dataRegion` honesty thread in the auth/bootstrap UX work, **TB-1467**, is the in-product face of the same rule). What actually forces region two: a signed-or-signable EU customer whose DPO requires EU processing — a *revenue-attached* forcing function, not an RFP checkbox; German/French public-adjacent buyers and EU healthcare hit this hardest. The trap to refuse: building multi-region speculatively — it's a permanent ~2× ops-surface tax (the exact opposite of the Q36 story) paid against hypothetical demand. The right pre-work is cheap and architectural: keep region assumptions out of schema and config in ways that would make a second stamp of the existing Terraform painful, and price the EU region into the deal that demands it.

**Backlog disposition:** Mostly covered — trust-center data-handling surface, **M-267/M-268**, **TB-1467**, residency-copy honesty rows (**TB-1255**). New: **GQ-16** — one-page canonical residency statement + second-region trigger criteria (§21).

## Part M — Growth mechanics and the honest mirror

### 39. Rank awareness channels by yield per founder-hour — and where will *this* founder overinvest?

**Answer.** For a category-creating product with no search volume, ranked by expected yield per founder-hour for this founder profile (expert practitioner, very fast technical writer): (1) **Practitioner long-form with proof artifacts attached** — highest yield *because* the writing is fast for this founder and the differentiated asset (real evidence packages, honest claim maps) is inimitable content; buyers of governance products are readers. (2) **Conference/meetup talks to architect audiences** — high trust-transfer per hour on stage, moderate prep cost, compounding via recordings; the honest-skeptic material (these very Q&A docs) is unusually good talk material. (3) **Community presence in architect watering holes** — cheap, slow, authentic; yield is real but diffuse. (4) **Open-sourcing components** — high cost, wrong buyer: developers star repos, architects' *procurement* buys governance; only worth it if a component (e.g., the extractor) doubles as a trust artifact. (5) **SEO on adjacent terms** — lowest yield; adjacent-term traffic converts poorly and the content burns hours competing with content farms. **The overinvestment prediction:** long-form writing — precisely because it's this founder's lowest-friction, highest-comfort activity, and the GTM backlog already shows it (~half of the entire P1 hour rollup is LinkedIn long-form). The failure mode isn't writing bad posts; it's writing *good* posts as a substitute for the higher-friction, higher-yield motions (talks, direct outreach) that involve rejection. The check is mechanical: yield-per-hour reviewed quarterly against pipeline touches, with a pre-committed rebalance rule.

**Backlog disposition:** Channel work exists throughout GTM backlog (LinkedIn slate, demo/outreach calendars). New: **GQ-17** — quarterly channel-yield review with a pre-committed rebalance trigger (owner decision aid; §21).

### 40. Which question did the vendor not ask — and what do the forty questions reveal?

**Answer.** **The unasked question: "What does month three of a paying customer look like?"** All forty questions interrogate *getting* the customer — differentiation, demo, pilot, procurement, pricing, liability, channels — and the kill-risk analysis (Q19) reasons about losing *deals*. Almost nothing interrogates *keeping* value alive after the pilot converts: who inside the account runs review four and five without the founder's gravity, what the renewal conversation cites, what expansion (more teams? more packages? governance seats?) even means, and what quiet non-usage looks like on a dashboard three months in. For a product whose moat *is* accumulated committed history (Q31), retention isn't one metric among many — it's the mechanism the entire compounding thesis depends on, and it's currently the least-instrumented part of the story. **What the questions' shape reveals:** a founder strongest at — and most comfortable in — adversarial *analysis*: claim honesty, threat modeling, procurement objections, technical defensibility. The blind spots cluster where the work is relational and longitudinal rather than analytical: post-sale success motion, expansion playbooks, and the unglamorous telemetry of whether people quietly stopped showing up. (Live-human *validation* work — cohorts, rehearsals, pilot-readiness tests — is deliberately routed to the GTM V1.1 backlog per owner decision, so its absence here is scope discipline, not a blind spot; the month-three question is different and unrouted.) The forty-first question to ask Grok, in a fresh session: *"Design the retention instrumentation and month-three success motion for this product, assuming the founder will underinvest in it."*

**Backlog disposition:** New — **GQ-18**: post-pilot retention/expansion signal definition + minimal instrumentation (§21). Complements **M-241/M-242** (happy-path stickiness) which covers the *first-session* return loop but not the month-three horizon.
